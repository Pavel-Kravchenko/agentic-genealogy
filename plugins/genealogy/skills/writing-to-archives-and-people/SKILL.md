# Writing to archives and to people

## Core principle

An institution and a private person are different addressees. An archive works from a formal request and
a detailed one helps it. A private person owes you nothing, and a long composed text asks a great deal of
them: the agent prepares the facts and the questions, the human writes the message in their own words and
says plainly that AI helped to prepare it.

## To a private person

Deliver this, and nothing that looks like a finished letter:

- **What to mention** - a short list of facts the writer may use (who we are to the person asked about,
  what we already hold, what we can offer in return).
- **Two or three questions**, each answerable in a sentence.
- No greeting, no closing, no signature, no paragraphs of prose to paste. The human writes the text, short.
- Do not advise sending long texts. Do not prepare interview "guides" or prompt sheets for conversations
  the family will hold themselves. A conversation is recorded only with the person's knowledge and
  consent; after it, transcribe, extract facts, list new questions.
- Never write a second time to someone who has closed the contact.

Questions for a family member who answers through someone else: give every question in full in every reminder,
as a numbered list that can be answered by number. A pointer to an earlier note does not reach them.
Record the answer as "stated by <role>, <date>".

## To an archive or registry

A request that gets a useful answer contains:

1. Who is asking and the relationship to the person sought (some archives require proof of kinship for
   recent records; ask the archive what it needs).
2. The person: name with spelling variants, year and place of the event **in the jurisdiction valid in
   that year** (province, uezd, volost, parish), confession.
3. The record genre wanted (baptism entry, marriage search, revision list, personal file) and the years.
4. What is already known and from which document, so that the archive does not repeat it.
5. The shelf marks, if the finding aids are online. A request with fond, inventory and file numbers is
   cheaper and faster than a thematic search.
6. How the answer may be sent.

Before sending: check by eye, on the page image, every number that goes into the request. OCR of
typewritten inventories changes digits.

When the reply comes:

- A negative reply is a closed door: enter it in the dead-ends ledger with its date and number, and do not
  ask the same archive the same question again.
- Read what the reply covers. A reply that covers another uezd or other years than those asked closes
  nothing for the question asked; say exactly what it covers.
- An archive may accept a correction to a request; a detailed list of corrections is appropriate there.
- Institutions answer in weeks to months. Keep a table: addressee | sent | what was asked | answered |
  what it closed.

## Hand-over block for the human

End your reply with a block the human can forward unchanged: polite, each point understandable alone,
room for an answer under each point, no internal identifiers of the project (person ids, script names,
commit hashes, jargon). Names, years and shelf marks that the addressee needs stay in.

Leave a marked placeholder for every fact you do not hold (the jurisdiction in the year of the event, the
confession, the kinship documents). Do not guess them: the human fills them in or you look them up first.

## Red flags

- A drafted email to a private person beginning with a greeting
- A message to a volunteer that lists everything the agent has read and done
- A questionnaire cut down to keywords (questions are given in full; only the letter is left to the human)
- A second request to an archive that has already answered no
